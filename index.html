    About this map

    A curated map of gravity mountain-biking spots worldwide: where to ride, what the lift costs, and whether it will be dry.

    • Spot-first. Each destination is one pin with a practical description of the riding, the lift, prices and opening dates. Trail traces are added when reliable, as a bonus rather than the foundation. On the ground, signs and local maps do the rest.
    • Filter to what you want. Narrow by spot type, riding style, difficulty, trail colour, or day-pass price, and search spots and traces by description.
    • Plan around the weather. Pick a date to see which spots are open and what the forecast is. Spots likely to be wet get a rain drop badge right on the map, so you can see at a glance where it is dry, with the threshold adapted to how well the terrain drains (rain-sensitive spots badge on light rain, rain-resilient ones only on heavy rain).
    • Take it offline. Download the whole map as KML, GPX or GeoJSON in one click, and open it in CoMaps, Organic Maps or OsmAnd. This site is just a convenient way to browse the same file.
    • Make it yours. Fork the repository and change whatever you like, easily done with coding agents. Send a pull request back to the main map, or just keep your own.
    • Tested and approved. Spots and trails carrying that mark were ridden in person by a contributor and confirmed as worthwhile.

    Why another MTB map?

    Finding a good spot usually means trawling a dozen resort sites, none of which tell you in one place what the trails are like, how the lift works and what it costs. And once you have picked somewhere, there is rarely a KML or GPX to drop into your GPS app.

    Then getting those trails onto your phone is its own chore: export them one by one, often behind a login screen or a paywall, then import each file into your GPS app. Here the whole map is a single download, no account needed.

    Sites like Trailforks are useful, but the good riding gets lost in the noise: filters do not narrow enough, and many mapped routes are uninteresting dirt roads or impractical hiking trails tagged for MTB, sometimes closer to alpinism than mountain biking. And these sites ask for a subscription to access their data, which is a barrier to sharing and offline use. This map is curated, free and open.

    Where the data comes from
    • Trail geometry from OpenStreetMap MTB route relations, pulled via Overpass and simplified, or from the operator's website. Indicative only: follow the signs on the ground, not the trace.
    • Lift prices, opening dates, daily hours and travel times compiled from the operators' own sites and Magic Pass, in July 2026. Where published daily hours could not be confirmed, the pin shows a typical regional estimate and says so explicitly.
    • Trail descriptions written by humans often assisted by an LLM, from riding and from operator trail maps.
    • Sources are linked per feature: each spot description and each trail trace includes links to the sources used for its description or geometry, so you can check the underlying information yourself.
    • Base map tiles from OpenStreetMap, OpenTopoMap (CC BY-SA) and CyclOSM.
    • Weather forecasts from Open-Meteo. A blue rain badge means at least 1 mm of precipitation or a 50% probability is forecast, or at least 5 mm fell the previous day; these are the thresholds for rain-sensitive spots (clay, dark forest soil, little sun). Untagged spots need a bit more rain, and rain-resilient spots (rocky, well-draining, sunny) only badge on heavy same-day rain.

    License: Map data under ODbL 1.0, because it derives from OpenStreetMap. Website code under MIT. Keep the attribution if you redistribute the files.

    Trail grading

    Trail colours are set by each operator, and the same colour means different things in different parts of the world. Europe uses four colours; North America splits the top end into black and double black instead; Japanese resorts mostly carry over their three-colour ski grading. Rough equivalences:

    Colour equivalences
    Level Europe North America Japan
    Easy Green Green circle Green
    Intermediate Blue Blue square Red
    Advanced Red Black diamond Black
    Very difficult Black Double black diamond Black
    Extreme Double black or orange Pro line, often orange -

    Treat this as indicative only: a red in one valley rides like a black in the next, and bike parks grade against their own network rather than a national standard. New Zealand uses numbered grades 1 to 6 instead of colours. The trail lines on this map keep the colour of the source data.

    Natural trails: T and E

    Ratings such as T5 / E2 combine two independent VTTour scales. T is how technically difficult the riding is; E is how bad a fall would be. T5 / E2 is therefore extremely difficult riding on a narrow trail with some obstacles, but without the cliff exposure of E3 or E4. Both assume dry conditions and stay subjective.

    Technical difficulty
    T1 Wide, smooth trail; gentle slope below roughly 20%; broad turns and no obstacles.
    T2 Wide trail; moderate 20-30% slope; fine loose stones, small obstacles and easy switchbacks.
    T3 Narrow and often steeper than 30%; roots, steps below 30 cm, medium scree and a few difficult switchbacks.
    T4 Narrow, steep and strongly off-camber; steps above 30 cm, large loose rock and repeated difficult or occasional trials-style turns.
    T5 Extreme gradients with numerous trials-style passages requiring advanced balance, wheel placement and low-speed control.
    Exposure and fall consequences
    E1 Low exposure: wide trail with no major hazards beside or below it.
    E2 Medium exposure: narrow trail with some obstacles that can worsen a fall.
    E3 High exposure: very narrow trail with many obstacles, steep slopes or small rock drops beside it.
    E4 Very high exposure: substantial cliffs or rock drops beside the trail; a fall can have critical consequences.

    Scale definitions adapted from VTTour's grading reference.
